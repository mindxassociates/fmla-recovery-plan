Mental health leave recovery guide

Your leave is for recovery—not for proving you can be productive.

A practical framework for using FMLA leave to stabilize, reconnect with treatment, restore basic rhythms, reduce work-related activation, and prepare for a sustainable return.

Six recovery priorities

Think in terms of consistent supports, not perfect performance. Your clinician’s treatment plan takes priority over this general guide.

01 / TREATMENT

Keep care at the center

Attend therapy, medication-management, medical, or other recommended appointments. Write down questions and changes you want to discuss.

02 / RHYTHM

Rebuild predictable days

Anchor the day with a reasonably consistent wake time, meals, medication as prescribed, and bedtime. A simple rhythm can reduce decision fatigue.

03 / BODY

Support the nervous system

Use gentle movement, time outdoors, regular meals, hydration, and sleep routines. Start small and adapt to your health and clinician’s guidance.

04 / BOUNDARIES

Reduce work activation

Mute nonessential work notifications and avoid repeatedly checking messages “just in case.” Use the leave for care and recovery, not informal remote work.

05 / CONNECTION

Stay connected without performing

Choose a small number of safe people. Ask for specific support: a walk, a meal, quiet company, transportation, or a check-in.

06 / OBSERVATION

Track patterns, not grades

Notice sleep, mood, anxiety, energy, concentration, appetite, and functioning. Bring meaningful trends to your care team.

Minimum viable day

When functioning is low, make the plan smaller.

Enough for today can be: take prescribed medication, drink water, eat something, attend or reschedule essential care, complete basic hygiene if possible, step outside or change rooms, and tell one trusted person that today is difficult. Recovery plans should flex downward when symptoms intensify.

Your weekly recovery reset

Once a week, spend 10–20 minutes reviewing the pattern. The purpose is to inform care—not to decide whether you are “recovering fast enough.”

Look ahead

Work boundaries during leave

FMLA is a legal leave framework, not a treatment. For eligible employees of covered employers, qualifying FMLA leave is job-protected. The details of communication and documentation vary by employer and situation.

Reduce unnecessary contact

Consider removing work email and chat from your phone, muting notifications, and using one designated channel for required leave administration.

Keep communication narrow

Respond to legitimate HR/leave-administration requests as needed, but you do not need to give coworkers a detailed account of your diagnosis or treatment.

Before you rush into a new job search

Wanting a different job can be understandable—especially when work contributed to distress. But interviewing immediately can quietly turn medical leave into another high-pressure performance cycle.

Career exploration is not the same as recovery

Browsing possibilities, writing down what you want to change, or discussing career values can be useful. A rapid cycle of applications, recruiter calls, interview preparation, and rejection or offer decisions can add deadlines, evaluation, uncertainty, and pressure while you are still trying to stabilize.

Ask what is driving the urgency

Before accelerating a search, ask: “Am I moving toward a healthier fit, or am I urgently trying to escape how I feel right now?” You do not have to solve your entire career during leave. Consider discussing the timing with your therapist or treating professional if interviewing is beginning to compete with treatment, sleep, basic routines, or recovery.

A more recovery-centered sequence

Stabilize → understand → explore → decide. First support basic functioning and treatment. Then identify what made the previous work situation difficult, what conditions you need to function sustainably, and which changes might come from boundaries or accommodations versus a different role. Job searching can then become a deliberate decision rather than an emergency exit.

Return readiness: assess function, not guilt

As leave progresses, discuss return-to-work timing with your treating professional. A return plan may include reasonable accommodations when applicable.

Daily stability

Can you reliably manage wake/sleep, meals, hygiene, medication, transportation, and basic responsibilities at a level compatible with work?

Cognitive load

How are concentration, memory, decision-making, frustration tolerance, and ability to shift between tasks?

Stress tolerance

What happens when you face deadlines, interpersonal friction, uncertainty, meetings, commuting, or sustained screen time?

Potential supports to discuss

Depending on your condition, job, and applicable law, options can include schedule changes, modified breaks, telework, reduced distractions, leave, or other individualized accommodations. Discuss what is clinically appropriate with your care team and what is feasible with your employer.

When recovery needs more support

Contact your clinician promptly if symptoms are worsening, you are unable to meet basic needs, medication problems arise, or your current treatment plan is not enough. If you are in immediate danger or thinking you may act on thoughts of suicide or self-harm, call or text 988 in the U.S., call 911, or go to the nearest emergency department.
